What AH Taught Me About Appreciation

David Lee, RD November 18, 2026 4 min read

Photo by Ian Stauffer on Unsplash

AH had never worked out before. She tried sports in high school but became demoralized and discouraged when people made fun of her. A couple of decades later, when she became a mom, she wanted to try a routine — so we met while I was still at a commercial gym.

We worked together twice a week for over two years. But it was the first six months that helped her appreciate what her body could do... and resent the people who had poked fun when she was younger.

If only they had seen her potential.

The simple things in her life got easier: carrying bags of groceries, playing with her kids, having the stamina to work full time and be a parent. And perhaps most importantly, she found a newfound appreciation for fitness. Whereas before she associated exercise with her negative childhood experiences, she now sees it as a positive driving force — one she wants to introduce to her kids.

Fitness is difficult and time-consuming. You need positive reasons to keep motivation levels up, and in my opinion, appreciation can be a powerful tool.

Early on as a trainer, I thought my responsibility was to work people out. While that's still true, I've become more convinced that one of my responsibilities is to help people appreciate what their bodies can do.

It's one thing to improve an exercise specific to a gym setting. It's another if that performance translates into the real world.

The best way I do this is to try to connect the gym to life — by asking if anything outside of the gym has gotten easier or more enjoyable.

A push-up in the gym is a push-up, but outside, it's the ability to get off the ground with more ease. A squat is a squat, but outside, it's the ability to get out of a chair. Step-ups are step-ups, but outside, they're the ability to climb stairs with less knee pain.

"How does it feel now compared to before?"

"How do you feel about the work you're doing here?"

"It's nice that you're able to enjoy these activities."

"Your kids have some more work to do if they want to win against their old man."

I often find that when clients like AH can appreciate the work they do — and the results — they stick to a routine longer than people who just "want to lose weight and get healthier." The goal is too abstract. Appreciation is concrete. It shows up every time you carry something heavy without struggling, or keep up with your kids without running out of breath.

What to do next

If you're struggling with motivation, see if appreciation can help. What are some things that have gotten easier over the time you've been working out? And if you're not working out currently — what is something that could improve if you started?

Cheers.

— David
